Stochastic optimization methods through statistical whitening · Part 2

A statistical view of Adam and Shampoo like methods

Whitening gives us a way to use gradient statistics. Choosing a covariance structure turns that idea into a practical optimizer.

Robert M. Gower, Tetiana Parshakova, and Paulo Orenstein · November 18, 2026

This post is based on Sections 3–5 of our paper, Stochastic optimization methods through statistical whitening.

Mean·Variance and covariance

The same idea, with less covariance

In the first post, we argued that we should track the mean and covariance of the gradients, then use whitened momentum:

wt+1=wt−ηtΣ^t−12m^t
(1)

How did we estimate these quantities? We solved a Gaussian KL projection problem:

arg minm,Σ≻0KL(𝒩(m^t,Σ^t⋆)‖𝒩(m,Σ))
(2)

The target mean and full covariance are given by the online updates:

Online update of mean and covariance

m^t=βm^t−1+(1−β)gtΣ^t⋆=βΣ^t−1+β(1−β)(gt−m^t−1)(gt−m^t−1)⊤
(3)

The problem is the size of this Σ^⋆ covariance. The weight matrix W∈ℝp×q of a given linear layer has pq parameters, so its full covariance needs O(p2q2) entries. This covariance is too large to store. To decrease the storage, we need to impose some structure on the covariance matrix.

Covariance structures for a p×q parameter matrix
StructureCovarianceEntries to store
DiagonalΣ=diag(σ)O(pq)
KroneckerΣ=C⊗RO(p2+q2)
Diagonal KroneckerΣ=diag(c)⊗diag(r)O(p+q)

Now we will choose one of the above structures, and we will use Σ∈S to denote a positive definite covariance with this structure. Then we solve the same KL projection (2), but restrict the covariance to S. Regardless of the choice of S, the mean update is still given by (3). After substituting this mean into the KL projection, both Gaussians have the same mean, so the remaining problem is a KL projection of the covariance alone:

Σ^t=arg minΣ∈SKL(𝒩(0,Σ^t⋆)‖𝒩(0,Σ))
(4)

The zero means in (4) emphasize that only the covariance remains to be fitted. The starred covariance is the full target from (3); the covariance without a star is its structured approximation. We do not actually construct the enormous full covariance: the formulas below compute the needed diagonals or factors directly. For readability, I leave damping and bias correction out of the displayed update formulas.

Diagonal covariance gives Centered Adam

The simplest choice is a diagonal. Solving (4) then keeps the diagonal entries of the full covariance estimate. Substituting this into whitened momentum (1) gives

Centered Adam

σ^t=βσ^t−1+β(1−β)(gt−m^t−1)⊙(gt−m^t−1)wt+1=wt−ηtm^tσ^t
(5)

Products and divisions in (5) are entrywise; σ^t stores variances, not standard deviations. This looks very much like Adam. The difference is that Adam uses the uncentered second moment, approximately σ^t+m^t⊙m^t, rather than the variance alone. Here we use the same β for both moments.

Does this distinction matter in practice? In our FineWeb experiments, Centered Adam performs about as well as Adam at both GPT-small and GPT-medium scales:

So perhaps we do not need to explain every detail of Adam to understand why an Adam-like method works. Centered Adam has a simple statistical interpretation: estimate the gradient variance, then whiten the mean. And for these experiments, Centered Adam works just as well as Adam. Though we'd need many more experiments before discarding Adam in favour of centered Adam. Also, Centered Adam is harder to initialize (see below).

Initialization matters for Centered Adam

Unlike the standard Adam initialization, we can't initialize Centered Adam with zero moment buffers. Because even after the first update, the variance is still zero, and we can't divide by zero. The fix is just to initialize at a non-zero variance. The two non-zero initializations we tried were a scaled identity, or just Adams uncentered second moment. Both worked well enough

In the experiments above, we use the AdamW denominator for the first 1–50 updates, then switch to the centered denominator at update 51. These 50 updates are included in the training budget. See Appendix D for the implementation.

Kronecker structure gives a Matrix Normal model

Using a diagonal covariance discards all correlations between coordinates. A richer choice is to use a Kronecker factorization Σ=C⊗R, with a row factor R∈ℝp×p and a column factor C∈ℝq×q, and ⊗ is the Kronecker product. For a matrix gradient Gt, this Gaussian model with a Kronecker factored covariance is also known as the Matrix Normal distribution:

vec(Gt)∼𝒩(vec(M),C⊗R)⇔Gt∼MN(M,R,C)
(6)

Here vec(M) stacks the columns of a matrix. Whitening with a Kronecker covariance can be done without ever forming the full covariance matrix, using the properties of the Kronecker product. The vector update (1) becomes

Wt+1=Wt−ηtR^t−12M^tC^t−12
(7)

We whiten on the left and on the right. The result has the shape of an inverse-square-root version of Shampoo. Ordinary Shampoo uses inverse fourth roots of its gradient Gram accumulators.

Fitting the factors

Substituting Σ=C⊗R into the covariance KL projection (4) gives a fitting problem for the two factors:

arg minR≻0,C≻0KL(𝒩(0,Σ^t⋆)‖𝒩(0,C⊗R))
(8)

We would like to solve (8) over positive definite R and C. Though the objective is not convex with respect to arithmetic averages of the factors, it is convex with respect to the geometric mean between matrices. This is called geodesic convexity. In fact, the objective is strictly geodesically convex in the covariance, so the optimal covariance product is unique. We can find it by alternating exact minimization over one factor and then the other.

KL-Shampoo, by Lin and colleagues, uses a closely related KL objective for an uncentered second-moment estimate. Our objective instead comes from jointly fitting the mean and covariance. The distinction changes both the residuals and the weights in the factor updates.

What does geodesically convex mean here?

Usual convexity looks at arithmetic averages (1−s)A+sB. For positive definite matrices, we can instead use the weighted geometric mean:

A#sB=A12(A−12BA−12)sA12
(9)

Here 0≤s≤1. At s=0 we get A, at s=1 we get B, and at s=12 we get their geometric mean. A function F is geodesically convex if these weighted geometric means stay in its domain and

F(A#sB)≤(1−s)F(A)+sF(B)
(10)

for every pair A, B in its domain and every s between 0 and 1. This is the usual convexity inequality with the arithmetic average replaced by the geometric mean.

For the matrix result and convergence proof, see Proposition 5.1 and Appendix E.1.

Diagonal Kronecker factors: the MND method

Using dense factors C and R will require some inverses and inverse square roots. To keep things simple, let's use diagonal factors R^t=diag(r^t) and C^t=diag(c^t). We call the resulting method based on these diagonal factors the Matrix Normal Diagonal method, or MND.

For this MND method, we update the mean estimate M^t using the same momentum method as always (3), but now reshaped as a matrix. As for the factors, we will take just one step of alternating minimization, which was enough in practice. That is, starting from the previous column factor c^t−1, we minimize (8) over r, which gives r^t. We then freeze r=r^t in (8) and minimize over c, giving c^t. This alternating minimization gives the following updates:

Update of diagonal factors

r^t,i=βr^t−1,i+β(1−β)q∑j=1q(Gt,ij−M^t−1,ij)2c^t−1,jc^t,j=βp(∑i=1pr^t−1,ir^t,i)c^t−1,j+β(1−β)p∑i=1p(Gt,ij−M^t−1,ij)2r^t,i
(11)

One more detail needs addressing. There is a scale ambiguity between these two factors. That is, multiplying one factor by a positive number and dividing the other by the same number leaves their Kronecker product unchanged. Ignoring it can lead to numerical issues, so we make sure their scales are balanced

How do we balance the factors?

We choose equal traces, rescaling the factors without changing their Kronecker product:

αt=∑j=1qc^t,j∑i=1pr^t,ir^t←αtr^tc^t←αt−1c^t
(12)
Further algorithmic details

We initialize M^0=0, r^0=1pp, and c^0=1qq. To avoid numerical overflow when inverting the factors, we floor the quantities being inverted or square-rooted at a positive ε, see Algorithm 1 in our paper for details.

Finally, we use the factors to whiten the mean gradient as in (3), which with diagonal factors is equivalent to:

Wt+1=Wt−ηtM^t⊘r^tc^t⊤
(13)

Having only diagonal factors is very cheap in operations and storage, since it uses only O(p+q) additional scalars.

MND in practice

At the selected learning rates on GPT-small, centered MND reaches a mean validation loss of 3.373, compared with 3.444 for KL-Shampoo and 3.494 for Distributed Shampoo. Centered MND also achieves the lowest mean final validation loss on GPT-medium over the tested learning-rate grids. The uncentered MND variant is close behind.

The result is encouraging: a much smaller covariance model can still make whitening useful. These are results for the diagonal-factor methods. Our dense-factor MN implementation was less competitive because of numerical difficulties with matrix inversion; see Section 6. The experiment figures include additional MND variants to test centering and the use of a separate covariance decay rate.
